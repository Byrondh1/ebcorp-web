Medium-Voltage Electrical Service Connection: What It Is, When You Need It, and What It Costs in Ecuador

When your company's demand outgrows what the low-voltage network can deliver, the utility requires a medium-voltage connection point. This guide explains what a medium-voltage service connection is, its components, the approval process, and real costs in Ecuador.

What is a medium-voltage electrical service connection?

A medium-voltage electrical service connection is the connection point between the distribution grid (13.2 kV or 22.8 kV, depending on the utility) and a customer's facility whose consumption level cannot be efficiently served at low voltage (120/240 V). Unlike a small residential or commercial service, a medium-voltage connection delivers power before it is stepped down, so the customer must provide its own distribution transformer.

In practice, this means the company shifts from buying electricity that is already usable, to buying it at medium voltage and stepping it down internally. That shift brings technical and economic advantages, but also new regulatory responsibilities.

When does your company need a medium-voltage connection?

Not every industrial business requires medium voltage. The utility typically requires — or recommends — it in these scenarios:

  • Contracted demand exceeds roughly 50-100 kW, depending on the low-voltage network capacity in the area
  • The plant has large motors or loads with heavy starting currents that cause voltage dips on the local low-voltage network
  • A growth plan will make the current connection insufficient within 1-2 years
  • The site is far from the nearest distribution transformer, so a low-voltage connection would mean excessive losses
  • Higher reliability and power quality are required (industrial parks, continuous-process plants)
Rule of thumb: If your company already has — or is evaluating — its own distribution transformer, that's a clear sign you need a medium-voltage service connection rather than a simple upgrade of an existing low-voltage service.

A medium-voltage connection project is not just a cable: it's a full system that must comply with NEC standards and the utility's technical requirements.

The utility approval process

Installing a medium-voltage service connection in Ecuador is not something you can execute without prior authorization. The typical process with the utility (CNEL, Empresa Eléctrica Quito, EEASA, or another, depending on the province) follows these steps:

  1. Application and feasibility study — The utility evaluates whether the nearby network can deliver the requested load
  2. Electrical design — A qualified electrical engineer prepares drawings that comply with NEC standards and the utility's requirements
  3. Plan approval — The utility reviews and approves the design before construction begins
  4. Construction and installation — Civil and electrical works are executed: structure, transformer, cell, and protection
  5. Technical inspection — The utility verifies the work matches what was approved
  6. Energization — Once the inspection passes, the system is energized and the final meter installed
Important: The full process, from application to energization, takes 4 to 10 weeks in most cases. Planning the connection well in advance avoids costly delays when starting up a new plant.

Cost depends mainly on transformer capacity, distance to the existing network, and whether the run is overhead or underground (more expensive, but safer and more aesthetic).

Common mistakes when planning a medium-voltage connection

  • Undersizing the transformer by planning only for current load instead of 5-10 year growth
  • Skipping the feasibility study before purchasing equipment, risking that the network can't support the requested load
  • Poor grounding, which compromises personnel safety and correct protection operation
  • Not budgeting for maintenance of the transformer and medium-voltage cell, which require periodic inspection

Frequently Asked Questions

What voltage counts as medium voltage in Ecuador?

Ecuadorian utilities (CNEL, Empresa Eléctrica Quito, EEASA, and others) typically operate at 13.2 kV or 22.8 kV, depending on the concession area. The exact level is confirmed by your local utility's feasibility study.

How long does approval for a medium-voltage service take?

Typically 4 to 10 weeks: feasibility study (1-2 weeks), design and plan approval (2-4 weeks), construction (2-4 weeks), and inspection plus energization (1-2 weeks).

Who owns the connection, the company or the utility?

It depends on the delivery point. The medium-voltage network up to the meter usually belongs to the utility; the transformer, the medium-voltage cell, and everything downstream of the delivery point are owned and maintained by the customer.

Does every industrial company need its own substation?

Not necessarily. A substation with its own transformer and medium-voltage cell is needed when demand exceeds available low-voltage capacity, generally above 50-100 kW, or when the utility requires medium-voltage metering for the contracted load.
