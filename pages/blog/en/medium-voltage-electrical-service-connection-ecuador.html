Medium Voltage Electrical Service Connections: A Complete Guide for Companies in Ecuador

What it is, when your company needs it, and how much a medium voltage electrical service connection costs. The connection that decides whether your plant gets the power its production actually needs.

What is a medium voltage electrical service connection?

A medium voltage electrical service connection is the link that brings power from the local utility's network — CNEL or EEQ, depending on the region — directly to a company-owned transformer on the client's premises, at voltage levels that in Ecuador typically range from 6.3 kV to 22 kV. Unlike a standard low-voltage connection, here the company receives "raw" power and takes on the responsibility of stepping it down, protecting it, and distributing it throughout its own facility.

In practice, this means the plant stops being a simple consumer and starts operating its own mini substation: transformer, switching cells, protection devices, and a medium-voltage metering system, all under its own technical and maintenance responsibility.

Key point: A medium voltage service connection isn't a cosmetic upgrade to your facility — it's the difference between running heavy machinery reliably and dealing with voltage drops, overheated cables, and penalties for low power factor.

When does your company need a medium voltage connection?

Most small businesses run comfortably on a low-voltage service (120/240 V). The jump to medium voltage becomes necessary once power demand outgrows what the low-voltage network can deliver reliably.

Low voltage vs. medium voltage

  • Low voltage (120/240 V): enough for offices, retail spaces, and small workshops with demand under roughly 50-60 kW.
  • Medium voltage (6.3-22 kV): required once installed load exceeds that range, or when the company runs large motors, industrial furnaces, compressors, or continuous production lines.

Signs your company needs one

  • The utility informs you that your projected demand exceeds what the local low-voltage network can supply
  • Frequent voltage drops when large motors start up
  • Expansion plans that will double or triple current consumption
  • Eligibility for medium-voltage industrial rates, generally cheaper per kWh than low-voltage rates
  • New machinery requiring high-capacity three-phase power

Components of a medium voltage service connection

A well-designed medium voltage connection combines several components that must work together and comply with Ecuador's NEC electrical code (available in Spanish):

1. Distribution transformer

Steps down medium voltage (say, 13.8 kV) to the plant's usable voltage (208/120 V or 480/277 V, depending on the design). Its kVA capacity is sized based on projected peak demand, not just current load.

2. Switching and protection cell

This is where the connection can be safely isolated for maintenance and switching operations, and where the medium-voltage fuses or breakers that protect the transformer against faults are located.

3. Medium-voltage metering system

The utility installs metering equipment (current and voltage transformers) that records consumption before step-down, which is the basis for industrial-rate billing.

4. Grounding system and differential protection

The grounding system of a medium-voltage substation must meet resistance values far stricter than a residential installation, given the risk of ground faults at these voltage levels.

Permitting process with the utility in Ecuador

Building a medium voltage electrical service connection isn't just construction work — it requires formal technical approval from the utility before the installation can be energized.

  1. Feasibility request — CNEL or EEQ is asked whether the local network has enough available capacity for the new medium-voltage connection point.
  2. Electrical design and single-line diagram — A licensed electrical engineer prepares the drawings for the service connection, the substation, and the protection scheme.
  3. Project approval — The utility reviews the design against current technical standards and the NEC code.
  4. Construction and installation — Civil and electrical work is carried out: transformer, switching cells, ground grid, and medium-voltage panel.
  5. Final inspection and energization — The utility inspects the finished installation and authorizes the final connection.
Warning: Energizing a medium voltage service connection without the utility's formal approval is illegal and extremely dangerous. Any fault in an unauthorized installation can void insurance coverage and create direct legal liability for the company.

Common mistakes when building a medium voltage service connection

The costliest mistakes we see in the field:
  • Undersizing the transformer — Only current demand is calculated, leaving no margin for the plant's future growth
  • Skipping the protection coordination study — Poorly calibrated fuses and relays that fail to isolate a fault in time
  • Insufficient grounding grid — Increases the risk of ground faults and endangers personnel and equipment
  • Starting construction before utility approval — Leads to delays, fines, and rework
  • No provision for future maintenance — Designs that leave no safe working clearance around the medium-voltage cell
