Industrial Grounding Systems in Ecuador: What They Are, How to Test Them and How Often

Grounding protects your workers and your equipment, yet almost nobody checks it. Learn to spot a deteriorated ground grid and what a good installation looks like.

What did the equipment that burned out the last time lightning struck near your plant cost you? And the more uncomfortable question: when was the last time anyone measured your industrial grounding system? In many Ecuadorian companies the ground grid is installed once, buried, and never checked again — until a machine fails or a worker gets shocked. This guide explains how it works, how to tell whether yours is failing and what to ask of whoever installs it.

What is a grounding system and what is it for?

A grounding system is the physical connection between the metal parts of your electrical installation and the earth, using electrodes (rods, grids, plates) and copper conductors. It does four jobs, not one:

  • Protects people: if equipment becomes energized by a fault, current finds a safe path to ground instead of passing through whoever touches it.
  • Lets protective devices operate: breakers and fuses need fault current flowing through a low-impedance path to trip in time.
  • Protects equipment: diverts surges and lightning energy into the ground.
  • Stabilizes the reference voltage for panels, drives and control electronics.
Keep in mind: grounding is not the same as “driving a rod into the soil.” A working system is designed around soil resistivity, available fault current and the type of load in the plant.

This matters especially in Ecuador's highlands, where frequent electrical storms, dry seasons and volcanic or rocky soils make ground resistance change a lot from one site to another. Two plants in the same province can need completely different designs, which is why copying a layout from another facility is a common and costly mistake.

Types of grounding in an industrial plant

An industrial facility contains several grounding systems that must be bonded together or kept separate according to the design:

Protective groundPeople, against indirect contactMotor frames, panels, metal structures
System groundElectrical system referenceTransformer or generator neutral
Lightning groundThe building and structure, against lightningDown conductors and external electrodes
Electronic groundPLCs, drives, measuring equipmentControl rooms, server rooms

Warning signs your grounding is failing

A deteriorated system rarely announces itself clearly. The most common alerts are:

  • Mild tingling shocks when touching frames or metal structures
  • Electronics, drives or PLCs that reset or burn out for no apparent reason
  • Nuisance tripping of residual-current protection
  • Visible corrosion on connectors, welds or exposed rods
  • Plant expansions that were never tied into the existing grid
  • No measurement records from the past few years

The most common cause in our region is corrosion and soil drying out: electrode resistance rises over time and through dry seasons, and what passed on installation day stops passing.

How grounding is tested and maintained

Resistance measurement

Testing an industrial grounding system is done with an earth tester, usually using the fall-of-potential method. The maximum acceptable value depends on the applicable standard and the type of installation, so the result must be compared against what the design and the NEC regulations in force in Ecuador require — not against a generic number.

Inspection and upkeep

  • Visually check connections, exothermic welds and equipotential bonding bars
  • Measure resistance at least once a year, ideally in the dry season
  • Log every reading to spot trends before a failure happens
  • Correct with soil improvement or additional electrodes when the value exceeds the limit

This check fits naturally inside a preventive maintenance plan for electrical networks or an industrial electrical audit.

Caution: testing or working on a ground grid while the plant is energized requires trained personnel and safety procedures. Never disconnect a ground conductor "just to test."

What drives the cost of a grounding system

There is no single price, because every plant is different. The factors that move the budget most are:

  • Soil resistivity: rocky, dry or sandy ground needs more electrodes, chemical treatment or larger grids than damp, clay-rich soil.
  • Fault current and the size of the substation or transformer.
  • Available area to bury the grid and the number of buildings or machines to bond together.
  • Lightning protection: if the plant needs it, the design of the external system is added.

Correcting a deteriorated ground grid almost always costs less than replacing a single drive or control panel damaged by surges, and far less than a workplace accident. If you already know your loads, our electrical project quote tool gives you a first estimate.

What to demand from whoever designs or installs your grounding

  • A soil resistivity study before design
  • Calculation report and drawings of the grid
  • Exothermic welds or certified connectors, not improvised splices
  • A signed measurement report using calibrated equipment
  • Equipotential bonding between all grounding systems in the plant
